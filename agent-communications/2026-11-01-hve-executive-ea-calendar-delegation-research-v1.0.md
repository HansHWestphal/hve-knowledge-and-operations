# Research View: Executive–EA Calendar Delegation in Microsoft Exchange

**Date:** November 1, 2026
**Author:** Luna, HVE CTO / Head Architect
**Audience:** HVE Knowledge Ops
**Status:** Research view; not HVE policy, an approved permission grant, or an agent-authority decision
**Scope:** Microsoft Outlook and Exchange Online patterns for an executive and Executive Assistant (EA), with related application-integration approaches

## Executive summary

Microsoft’s Exchange model is not one “delegate” permission. It combines calendar-folder access, delegate status, meeting-request routing, mailbox access, and the identity under which the assistant sends. Its strength is that an executive can give an assistant practical authority without necessarily granting access to the entire mailbox. Its main drawback is that controls are distributed across Outlook, Exchange permissions, and—when automated—Microsoft Graph. A partially configured relationship can look like delegation while missing important behavior.

For a human EA managing an executive’s calendar, the clearest native pattern is to configure the EA as a delegate in Outlook on the executive’s primary calendar. Then choose meeting-request delivery, decide separately whether private items should be visible, and make an explicit choice between “Send on Behalf” and “Send As.” Add full mailbox access only when the EA’s responsibilities extend beyond calendar work. This document describes Microsoft’s design as a research reference; it does not adopt it as HVE policy or authorize any access.

## How the executive–EA relationship works

Microsoft’s intended human workflow is **Outlook delegate access**. A delegate can view and edit non-private events, receive meeting requests and responses, and respond on the executive’s behalf. This is more than sharing a calendar: delegate setup coordinates calendar access with meeting-request handling. Microsoft recommends creating or managing the complete relationship in Outlook while the calendar owner is signed in, rather than assembling it solely from Exchange folder-permission commands. See [Exchange Online’s explanation of delegate access](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/collaboration-exo/shared-calendars/understand-calendar-delegate-permissions#delegate-access) and [Microsoft’s Outlook sharing and delegation guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/graph/outlook-share-or-delegate-calendar).

Private appointments are a deliberate exception. Delegates do not automatically see private-item details; the owner can separately permit that visibility. This supports confidentiality, but “the EA is a delegate” does not necessarily mean “the EA can see everything.” The owner should grant private-item access only when it is needed. The Exchange article explains how the `Delegate` and `CanViewPrivateItems` flags relate to calendar permissions.

The executive can also determine **who receives meeting requests and who may respond**. Microsoft Graph documents three delivery choices: send requests to delegates only; send them to delegates while giving the principal an informational copy; or send them to both so either can respond. The first makes the EA the operational gatekeeper; the last keeps the executive as a co-recipient. See [Graph’s meeting-request delivery settings](https://learn.microsoft.com/graph/outlook-share-or-delegate-calendar#get-or-set-mailbox-setting-to-receive-meeting-requests-and-responses).

## Other permission patterns—and when they fit

**Calendar sharing or folder permissions** are narrower alternatives. Exchange roles range from free/busy visibility to read-only detail and editing. For example, `Reviewer` can view items, while `Editor` can create and manage them. This is useful when someone needs visibility or limited calendar maintenance, but folder access alone does not necessarily establish meeting-request delivery or the ability to send on the owner’s behalf. Microsoft’s guidance is explicit that an `Editor` entry alone is not a complete delegate relationship: the delegate flag and related settings matter too. Use Exchange Online PowerShell for folder-level administration and validation when that is the actual requirement—not as a substitute for configuring a complete EA relationship. See [Exchange’s calendar roles](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/collaboration-exo/shared-calendars/understand-calendar-delegate-permissions#calendar-permission-roles) and [the guidance for managing permissions and delegates](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/collaboration-exo/shared-calendars/manage-calendar-permissions#manage-delegates).

**Mailbox permissions and sender identity** are separate decisions from calendar access. Full Access opens a mailbox and permits work with its contents, but does not itself permit sending from it. “Send on Behalf” makes the assistant’s role visible to recipients; “Send As” makes a message appear to come from the executive alone. Outlook can configure Send on Behalf as part of delegate setup, but administrators should still review mailbox-level rights separately from calendar-folder rights. See [Exchange’s explanation of calendar and mailbox permissions](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/collaboration-exo/shared-calendars/understand-calendar-delegate-permissions#calendar-permissions-and-mailbox-permissions).

The trade-off is between convenience, scope, and transparency. Full Access may be appropriate if the EA also handles the executive’s email, but it is excessive for calendar-only work. Send As may match an organization’s communication practice, but recipients cannot tell from the From line that the EA sent the message. Send on Behalf preserves that distinction. By default, sent messages may be stored in the assistant’s Sent Items; Exchange can be configured to save copies in the executive’s mailbox too. See [Microsoft’s sent-items guidance for delegators and assistants](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/recipients-in-exchange-online/manage-user-mailboxes/automatically-save-sent-items-in-delegator-s-mailbox).

A **shared mailbox** is designed for a group using a common identity, such as a support address—not primarily for an assistant acting for a named executive. Users access it through their own accounts after being granted permissions; Full Access and Send As/Send on Behalf are distinct grants. This can suit a role-owned or team-owned calendar whose continuity should not depend on one person. It is not a direct substitute for an executive–EA relationship: the mailbox represents a team or function rather than the executive-principal relationship. See [Exchange Online’s shared-mailbox permission guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/collaboration-exo/shared-mailboxes#which-permissions-should-you-use).

## Application and legacy integration approaches

For applications, **Microsoft Graph** exposes sharing and delegation permissions, calendar operations, and meeting-request delivery settings. But the sharing or delegation relationship is established first—through an Outlook invitation and acceptance or by explicitly adding the calendar—before an application manages it. Graph integrations must select suitable delegated or application permissions; Microsoft advises using the least privilege needed for each operation and the v1.0 endpoint for new integrations. See [Graph’s calendar-sharing and delegation guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/graph/outlook-share-or-delegate-calendar#before-you-begin).

Microsoft’s **EWS** documentation describes three developer-oriented patterns: add a delegate, set folder permissions directly, or use impersonation. Delegation models a person acting on behalf of an owner; folder permissions grant access without necessarily granting send rights; impersonation supports service-scale access and makes messages appear to come from the mailbox owner, without showing the service identity to recipients. That may be useful for a service, but is a poor fit for a human EA relationship where attribution matters. Microsoft points new integrations toward Graph rather than treating older EWS guidance as the default. See [EWS’s comparison of delegate access, folder permissions, and impersonation](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/client-developer/exchange-web-services/delegate-access-and-ews-in-exchange#how-does-delegate-access-work).

## Benefits, limitations, and design takeaway

The model’s benefits are scoped access, explicit privacy controls, distinct sender identities, and configurable ownership of meeting requests. Its costs are configuration complexity and operational fragility: rights can be split between calendar-folder settings and mailbox-level settings; private-item access must be handled separately; and hybrid on-premises/Exchange Online deployments can have synchronization and client limitations. Microsoft’s [hybrid delegation overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/troubleshoot/exchange/send-emails/overview-delegation-office-365-hybrid#delegates) describes how cross-forest moves and differing Outlook behavior can disrupt delegation.

As a research pattern, the most coherent setup for a typical executive and EA is Outlook-native delegation on the executive’s primary calendar, private-event visibility granted only if required, an explicit choice of meeting-request routing, and a deliberate decision between Send on Behalf and Send As. Full mailbox access should follow actual job needs, not be assumed from calendar responsibility. Use a shared mailbox for a team-owned calendar, and Graph when software needs to manage an already-established relationship. This keeps calendar authority, privacy, and sender attribution as separate decisions instead of treating “delegate” as an all-or-nothing grant.

**HVE boundary:** This is a Microsoft-product research view only. It does not create an HVE delegation policy, change any agent’s authority, or approve calendar or mailbox permissions.
